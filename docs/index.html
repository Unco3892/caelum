Matchability-Aware Conformal Prediction for Open-Ended Language Model Generation

Ilia Azizi

University of Lausanne, Switzerland
BegooAI, Switzerland

Paper Poster Code BibTeX

TL;DR

  1. Calibrated Aleatoric-Epistemic Language Uncertainty Modeling (CAELUM) is a method for creating semantic prediction sets. It samples answers to a question under different clarifying prompts, clusters them semantically and then uses uncertainty to form a prediction set. We want to separate input ambiguity (aleatoric uncertainty) from gaps in the model’s knowledge (epistemic uncertainty).
  2. Matchability, the proportion of test questions for which the reference answer matches a sampled cluster, is a ceiling on coverage. CAELUM reached that ceiling in 16 of the 18 main model–dataset combinations.
  3. A classifier predicts which questions are matchable before generating answers. Across 30 model–dataset combinations with 4–32B generators, the 78 evaluations that use the learned classifier give average coverage of 0.74 on questions predicted as matchable and 0.65 on all questions.
  4. CAELUM and the aleatoric-only baseline (Vanilla CP) have similar average prediction set sizes across data splits. If all candidate values of λ give the same average prediction set size on the selection split, the fitted value λ⋆ does not identify the source of uncertainty.

Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) often do not produce calibrated uncertainty estimates for their answers. We propose CAELUM, a framework that builds semantic prediction sets while separating aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty. CAELUM samples answers using an ensemble of clarification prompts, clusters them by semantic equivalence, and estimates the aleatoric and epistemic components via kernel-based von Neumann entropy before calibrating nonconformity scores with split conformal prediction. We use a weight wλ(x) = 1 + Uale(x) + λ Uepi(x) to control for the contribution of the epistemic uncertainty of each instance and fit a coefficient λ⋆ to minimize prediction set size. When the objective is nonflat and selection is stable across splits, λ⋆ could be a diagnostic, where large values may indicate epistemic dominance (retrieve, abstain) and λ⋆ → 0 aleatoric dominance (clarify). For individual queries, a log ratio R(x) compares the weighted epistemic component with the aleatoric component. Theoretically, we demonstrate that the coverage is capped by matchability, the fraction of items whose reference answer appears in any sampled cluster. Empirically, this matchability can be predicted from the original question features using a lightweight classifier. We use those predictions to define a stratum and derive a conformal coverage bound within it. Across 30 LLM and dataset combinations (4–32B), mean coverage is 0.74 in the learned stratum versus 0.65 overall, averaged over the 78 evaluations using the learned classifier.

1Sampled support and matchability

For each question, CAELUM samples answers from the model and groups answers with similar meanings into semantic clusters. A prediction set is a subset of these clusters. Matchability records whether the reference answer matches at least one sampled cluster under a fixed evaluation rule.

A question is matchable if M(x, y*) = 1. Over a population of questions, the matchability rate is π = ℙ(M = 1). In our 18 main cells, it ranges from 0.21 (Gemma-4 on SimpleQA) to 1.00 (Qwen3-8B on AmbigInst).

If the reference answer matches no sampled cluster, even the collection of all clusters will miss it. Coverage over a population is therefore at most the matchability rate π, no matter what threshold is used in the conformal step.

For this question, we found Uale = 0.270 and Uepi = 0.392, and CAELUM returned both clusters.

The fitted weight for this cell is λ⋆ = 47.4, which gives w ≈ 19.9. For any τ̂ ≥ 0.037, Madrid is included in the set. The set still misses the reference.

2Building prediction sets

In our experiments, the model produces five clarifications for each question and ten answers to each clarification. Each question therefore gets 50 answers.

We use the two-stage sampling from input clarification ensembling (ICE) by Hou et al. (2024), and the kernel entropy and decomposition from the spectral framework of Walha et al. (2026).
  1. SampleThe model produces n clarifications of each question. For factual questions, these are rewordings; for ambiguous ones, they are intended to disambiguate the question. It then produces m answers for each clarification.
  2. ClusterWe cluster the nm answers by the similarity of their sentence embeddings. The cluster frequencies pk define an empirical distribution over candidate meanings.
  3. Measure uncertaintyThe kernel von Neumann entropy of all pooled answers gives Utotal. Averaging the entropy within each clarification gives Uepi. The residual is Uale = max(Utotal − Uepi, 0).
  4. CalibrateWe divide the cumulative cluster score by wλ(x) = 1 + Uale(x) + λ Uepi(x). One split selects λ; a separate split fits the conformal threshold τ̂.
  5. PredictFor a new question, consider clusters in decreasing order of pk. Include each cluster when the mass of all preceding clusters is at most η = min{τ̂ w, 1}.

Figure 2 gives the overview from our paper, with the two calibration constants presented as controls. The first question is ambiguous and has three valid answers. The second and third are knowledge gaps: for the second, the reference was sampled; for the third, it never was.

We use a deterministic APS-style score following Romano et al. (2020). It is the mass of clusters ranked above the one containing the reference. The score is 0 when the selected reference cluster is first in the ranking.

Within a clarification

The epistemic component captures the variability in answers that remains once we condition on a clarification. It gives a modest empirical signal for detecting failures of the sampled support.

Between clarifications

The aleatoric residual captures variability introduced by the clarification ensemble. It depends on the clarifications themselves and is not a verified measure of ambiguity in the original question.

Normalization makes the calibration threshold depend on λ. Without normalization, the calibration scores are independent of λ. Set size can only increase as λ grows, so the search returns λ⋆ = 0 under our rule of choosing the smallest weight in a tie. Even with normalization, discrete sets can stay the same across much of the λ grid. A coefficient fitted to a flat objective cannot identify an uncertainty regime. Even when the objective distinguishes weights, an interpretation requires stability across splits.

There are 4,011 values of λ in the grid: ten linear points in [0, 0.09] and 4,001 log-spaced points in [0.1, 100]. A separate 30-cell audit used a sparse grid. Across its weights up to 100, set size changed for only about 3.6% of test items.

3Coverage bounds

A logistic classifier, m̂, takes features of a question and predicts whether it is matchable, prior to generating an answer. This classifier is trained on one data split and then fixed. Mondrian calibration uses only the calibration questions that the classifier predicts will be matchable.

Features of the question include its all-mpnet-base-v2 embedding, its character and token length, the number of numeric and capitalized tokens, whether it includes a year and its first question word.

Let ρ be the proportion of questions in this subset for which there is no corresponding reference in the support. Assuming exchangeability of the calibration and test examples, including their generations and any score randomization, and fixed matching pipeline and score, Theorem 1 gives us:

1 − α − ρ  ≤  ℙ(y* ∈ 𝒞(x)  |  m̂(x) = 1)  ≤  1 − ρ.

The lower bound allows for false matchability and conformal error. The upper bound is the proportion of matchable questions in this subset. In its group, a classifier with ρ = 0.20 caps coverage at 0.80 at any given conformal threshold.

How does the support-conditional proposition differ?

Proposition 1 produces calibration of matchable questions and guarantees at least 1 − α coverage, given matchability. This is calibration over matched examples, different from the calibration in our main experiment. In general, the stratum considered in Theorem 1 can include some unmatched questions. So in that theorem's bound we include ρ. If our filter has ρ = 0, we could guarantee nominal coverage of questions in its group. And if in addition it included all matchable questions, we would recover Proposition 1.

What must be fixed before calibration?

To get the guarantee in Theorem 1, we would fix the scoring rule, λ, classifier, encoder, clustering threshold and reference-matching rule independently of the calibration and test points. They could be built on a separate data set and then fixed. Taken across a stratum, this gives us a guarantee. It is not a guarantee for every question.

Matchability prediction

Including Qwen3-32B and Qwen3-14B on these six datasets means we have five generators and 30 cells in total. Across four seeds of the split, mean coverage in the predicted-matchable stratum of our 78 learned classifiers was 0.74 ± 0.15. Across all test questions in the same rows, it was 0.65 ± 0.17. On these rows, the calibration split AUC of the classifier was 0.67 ± 0.08. Rows in which the training or calibration data had only one class or in which its AUC was under 0.55 are shown separately. These are oracle-fallback reference results. Neither coverage reaches our target of 0.90.

Taken across six generator comparisons, the classifier on the questions of each generator achieves a mean AUC of 0.78 on the target labels and 0.80 on the source labels. Each generator sees the same questions. Where a question appears in more than one dataset, its label can be different in each.

Set size and uncertainty scores

Across the four split seeds, the aleatoric-only baseline and CAELUM each produce about 2.21 clusters per set. Differences in set size between them within each cell are smaller than variation across seeds.

For ranking failures of the sampled support, Uepi beats Utotal on 15 of 17 non-degenerate cells across seeds. The simpler Uepi signal also beats R(x) on 16 of 18 cells in the separate routing analysis.

6Limitations

  1. The learned classifier is weak on some cells with low matchability, including PopQA. Its precision, recall, selection rate and induced ρ must be evaluated at the intended operating point. AUC alone does not establish a useful coverage stratum.
  2. Numerical matchability depends on semantic clustering and on the reference matcher. The judge audit examines heuristic-unmatched cases and identifies additional matches. That audit tests sensitivity to missed matches, and it leaves the heuristic-positive cases unchecked.
  3. The uncertainty decomposition measures the clarification pipeline. It does not establish why a model missed an answer, and an unstable λ or R(x) cannot prescribe retrieval, abstention or clarification.
  4. Abstention pilots add an outcome to the label space. Their augmented coverage must be reported alongside ordinary semantic coverage and the cost of abstaining. They do not repair the pre-generation classifier, which reads only the question text.
  5. Our experiments concern English short-answer QA with references that can be checked. Longer text with several claims, and generation over several turns, would need a separate formulation of coverage.
